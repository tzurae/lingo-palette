# Contextual second-language vocabulary retention

## Scope and reading rule

This note is intended to ground review design in original experiments, not to prescribe a universal learning schedule. Sources were included only when the original article or a first-party publication record could be read and linked directly. “Receptive” means retrieving or recognizing meaning from an L2 form; “productive” means retrieving or producing the L2 form from a meaning or L1 cue. Results below are bounded to each experiment’s participants, materials, intervention, and test delay.

## Evidence

### 1. Spacing and review timing

#### Contextual encounters spaced within a session

**Nakata & Elgort (2021), contextual learning from informative sentences** — [DOI and publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658320927764)

- **Intervention.** Learners met each of 48 pseudowords in three informative English sentences. In the massed condition all three sentences appeared together and prompted one meaning inference followed by feedback; in the spaced condition the sentences appeared about 25 minutes apart and prompted three inference-and-feedback cycles, with time on task equated across conditions. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658320927764)]
- **Population.** The sample was 66 Japanese undergraduate and postgraduate learners of English, mostly English majors, with an average Vocabulary Size Test estimate of 8,698 word families. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658320927764)]
- **Measured outcome.** On immediate and two-day tests, the spaced condition scored higher on explicit meaning recall and meaning–form matching; the conditions did not differ on the semantic-priming measure of tacit knowledge. The experiment therefore supports a short-delay advantage for this *spaced contextual-inference package* on its explicit measures, not an advantage on every kind of vocabulary knowledge. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658320927764)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** Spacing was confounded with the number of inference/retrieval and feedback cycles (three versus one); the delay was only two days; and the sample, pseudowords, and controlled informative sentences do not establish the same effect for beginners, authentic text, known words, or longer retention. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658320927764)]

#### Massed versus spaced paired-associate retrieval

**Nakata & Suzuki (2019), spacing and semantic relatedness** — [open original article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000219)

- **Intervention.** Participants studied 48 low-frequency English–Japanese pairs four times. Repetitions were either concentrated in sequential blocks or distributed through blocks of other items; after initial presentation, encounters required receptive translation with corrective feedback. Half of the words were semantically related coordinates and half were matched unrelated words. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000219)]
- **Population.** The experiment enrolled 133 Japanese university students; the reported analyses excluded participants according to prespecified aptitude-data and performance criteria. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000219)]
- **Measured outcome.** Massing produced higher accuracy during learning, but spacing produced higher receptive-translation accuracy after one week for both related and unrelated words. Spacing also reduced within-category substitution errors for related words on the delayed test; the retention advantage was larger for unrelated items in this experiment. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000219)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** The outcome was only L2-to-L1 translation, the treatment was decontextualized paired-associate learning, and retention was followed for one week. The result does not show the same schedule is optimal for production, contextual use, or longer intervals. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000219)]

#### Equal versus expanding gaps

**Nakata (2015), expanding and equal spacing** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825)

- **Intervention.** One hundred twenty-eight Japanese college students learned 20 English–Japanese word pairs while spacing *type* (equal or expanding) and spacing *amount* (massed, short, medium, or long) were manipulated. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825)]
- **Measured outcome.** The amount of spacing produced large differences on the study’s vocabulary tests, whereas expanding gaps had only a limited, though statistically significant, advantage over equal gaps. The experiment’s central practical contrast was therefore introducing an interval at all, not a decisive win for a finely shaped expanding schedule. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** This was a controlled English–Japanese paired-associate study rather than contextual reading, and its comparison does not establish that expanding gaps outperform equal gaps for arbitrary item difficulty, learner populations, or target retention horizons. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825)]

#### Weeks between reviews and years of retention

**Bahrick et al. (1993), long-term foreign-language maintenance** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x)

- **Intervention.** Four participants learned and relearned 300 English–French or English–German pairs in 13 or 26 sessions separated by 14, 28, or 56 days, with retention checked one, two, three, or five years after training. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x)]
- **Measured outcome.** Longer intervals slightly slowed acquisition but substantially improved later retention; 13 sessions 56 days apart produced retention comparable to 26 sessions 14 days apart in this nine-year investigation. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** The sample comprised only four highly involved participants, including the investigators, and used repeated word-pair relearning. The study is unusually informative about multi-year retention but is not a population estimate or evidence for assigning every learner a 56-day gap. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x)]

### 2. Retrieval practice and how much to review

#### Retrieval embedded in contextual reading

**Barcroft (2015), retrieval opportunities while reading** — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12139) · [first-party abstract record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1065763)

- **Intervention.** Seventy-four intermediate Spanish-speaking learners of English read a passage in which five targets occurred three times. The control group always saw an L1 translation; the retrieval group saw it on the first occurrence, then attempted to retrieve and write the English word on occurrences two and three. [[Primary publication record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1065763)]
- **Measured outcome.** Across L1-to-L2 and L2-to-L1 posttests, the retrieval package produced 51% more measured vocabulary learning than the always-show-translation condition in this experiment. [[Primary publication record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1065763)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** Only five items were studied, and the publication abstract does not establish durable retention on a delayed test. The manipulation combined retrieval, writing, and removal of later translation display, so it does not isolate a pure retrieval effect. [[Primary publication record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1065763)]

#### Continuing retrieval after the first successful answer

**Karpicke & Roediger (2008), repeated retrieval of foreign word pairs** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)

- **Intervention.** Forty university students learned 40 Swahili–English pairs through alternating study and test periods. Once a pair had first been recalled, it was either kept in or removed from subsequent study and/or test periods. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)]
- **Measured outcome.** Repeated study after the first correct recall did not improve one-week recall, whereas keeping successfully recalled pairs in later tests produced a large delayed-recall advantage in this experiment. Learners’ predictions did not track their later performance. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** The task used decontextualized Swahili–English pairs, a small university sample, and one delayed free-recall measure. It supports continued retrieval under those conditions, not indefinite drilling or a specific contextual-review algorithm. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)]

#### Repetition count versus time efficiency

**Nakata (2017), within-session retrieval frequency** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263116000280)

- **Intervention.** Ninety-eight Japanese learners studied 16 English–Japanese word pairs with one, three, five, or seven successful retrievals in a session; posttests included a delay longer than two weeks. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263116000280)]
- **Measured outcome.** Five and seven retrievals produced higher raw posttest scores than one and three regardless of test timing, but one retrieval produced the largest gain when time on task was controlled. More repetitions therefore increased per-item retention while reducing learning efficiency per unit of study time in this design. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263116000280)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** The study used only 16 decontextualized pairs and within-session retrieval. Its time-normalized result is a breadth-versus-depth tradeoff, not evidence that one retrieval is sufficient for durable knowledge or contextual use. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263116000280)]

### 3. Receptive versus productive learning and recall

**Webb (2005), glossed reading versus sentence production** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263105050023)

- **Intervention and population.** In two experiments, Japanese university EFL learners met target words either by reading three glossed sentences or by producing a sentence containing the word. Experiment 1 included 66 learners and equalized study time; Experiment 2 included 49 learners and allowed each task the time required, so writing took longer. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263105050023)]
- **Measured outcome.** Ten immediate measures tested receptive and productive knowledge of orthography, meaning–form links, association, syntax, and grammatical functions. Equal time favored glossed reading overall; allowing natural completion time favored sentence production. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263105050023)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** Task direction was inseparable from the activities (reading versus writing), and Experiment 2 also varied time on task. With no delayed test, the findings concern immediate multidimensional word knowledge rather than long-term retention. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263105050023)]

**Webb (2009), receptive versus productive word-pair learning** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688209343854)

- **Intervention and population.** Japanese EFL students learned English word pairs either receptively (L2 form to L1 meaning) or productively (L1 meaning to L2 form). [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688209343854)]
- **Measured outcome.** Productive study produced larger gains in receptive and productive orthography and in productive meaning, syntax, and grammatical-function knowledge; receptive study produced larger gains in receptive meaning. The test direction and knowledge dimension changed which condition looked better. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688209343854)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** These were decontextualized pairs, and the publisher abstract does not report delayed retention. The study argues against treating “word known” as one scalar but does not establish that productive practice is always superior. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688209343854)]

### 4. Contextual variation and multiple encounters

#### Same frequency, different numbers of texts

**Frances, Martin, & Duñabeitia (2020), contextual diversity** — [open original article](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70922-1)

- **Intervention.** Eight pseudowords each appeared exactly eight times, distributed across one, two, four, or eight fictional texts. Participants read for comprehension without intentional word study; 44 read in their native Spanish and 44 in English as a foreign language. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70922-1)]
- **Population.** The 88 adults were native Spanish speakers; those assigned to English met at least a B2 English threshold, so the foreign-language evidence is from relatively proficient readers. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70922-1)]
- **Measured outcome.** With frequency fixed at eight, distribution across more texts improved same-session form recall, recognition, and word–image meaning matching; the diversity effect did not interact with language condition, and passage-comprehension accuracy was not reduced. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70922-1)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** Greater text diversity also increased within-session spacing, leaving those mechanisms partly confounded. The study used eight inferable pseudowords, proficient learners, and tests shortly after learning, so it cannot establish long-term effects or the same pattern for beginners and opaque contexts. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70922-1)]

#### Increasing encounter count in context

**Webb (2007), one, three, seven, or ten encounters** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/aml048)

- **Intervention and population.** One hundred twenty-one Japanese EFL learners encountered pseudowords substituted into authentic texts one, three, seven, or ten times. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/aml048)]
- **Measured outcome.** Ten tests sampled receptive and productive orthography, meaning–form connection, grammatical functions, syntax, and association. Additional encounters produced gains in at least one knowledge dimension at every tested repetition level; ten encounters produced substantial but incomplete knowledge. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/aml048)]
- **Limitations/generalizability.** The posttests followed the treatment without a delayed-retention test, and controlled pseudoword substitution differs from encountering naturally unknown words. The experiment does not establish a universal “ten encounters” mastery threshold. [[Primary source](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/aml048)]

## Evidence synthesis for review decisions

- **Spacing:** In controlled L2 tasks, distributing encounters improved delayed explicit recall relative to massing, even when massing looked better during practice; however, effective interval size varied with the tested horizon, and contextual experiments sometimes changed retrieval count or contextual diversity along with spacing. [[Nakata & Elgort, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267658320927764); [Nakata & Suzuki, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000219)]
- **Schedule shape:** The direct L2 comparison found a much larger effect from adding spacing than from choosing expanding rather than equal gaps. The only multi-year foreign-vocabulary experiment favored wider gaps but had four participants. [[Nakata, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825); [Bahrick et al., 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x)]
- **Retrieval:** Requiring recall rather than repeatedly displaying an answer improved later performance in both contextual-reading and word-pair experiments, but the contextual manipulation bundled retrieval with writing and reduced answer exposure. [[Barcroft, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12139); [Karpicke & Roediger, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408)]
- **Selection and stopping:** One correct answer was not the end of useful retrieval in Karpicke and Roediger’s one-week task, while Nakata’s time-controlled analysis showed a real opportunity cost to many repetitions in one session. Together these experiments identify a depth-versus-breadth decision; they do not specify one optimal stopping rule. [[Karpicke & Roediger, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408); [Nakata, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263116000280)]
- **Recall direction:** Receptive and productive practice developed different measurable components of word knowledge, and time on task could reverse the aggregate winner. A review history should therefore not collapse recognition and production into one interchangeable success signal. [[Webb, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263105050023); [Webb, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688209343854)]
- **Contexts and encounters:** More encounters broadened immediate word knowledge, and holding frequency constant while distributing encounters across more texts improved immediate performance in proficient L2 readers; the latter result cannot cleanly separate context diversity from within-session spacing. [[Webb, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/aml048); [Frances et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-70922-1)]

## Product implications

These are design inferences, not claims made or tested by the studies.

- **[Product inference]** Schedule a successfully reviewed item again after a real gap rather than immediately repeating it by default; treat strong massed-session performance as weak evidence of durable retention.
- **[Product inference]** Prefer a simple, inspectable spacing rule before optimizing equal versus expanding micro-schedules; the L2 evidence for the exact gap shape is smaller than the evidence for introducing spacing.
- **[Product inference]** Tie target gaps to the intended retention horizon and observed learner performance rather than hard-coding the 14-, 28-, or 56-day intervals from a four-person longitudinal study.
- **[Product inference]** Do not retire an item after its first correct retrieval. Require evidence from retrieval on a later occasion, but cap same-session repetitions so difficult items do not consume the entire review budget.
- **[Product inference]** Select more review for failed or effortful retrievals and less immediate review for fluent successes, while preserving at least one later retrieval check; none of the reviewed experiments validates a particular adaptive formula.
- **[Product inference]** Ask for retrieval before revealing the answer, then provide corrective feedback. When the prompt comes from a reading context, distinguish this combined intervention from evidence for “pure” testing effects.
- **[Product inference]** Track receptive meaning access and productive form recall separately. A correct L2-to-meaning recognition should not automatically mark meaning-to-L2 production as mastered.
- **[Product inference]** Match the review direction to the learner’s goal: include productive prompts for speaking/writing goals and receptive prompts for reading/listening goals, rather than ranking one direction as universally harder or better.
- **[Product inference]** Re-present a target across different meaningful sentences or passages when available, while keeping the lexical sense stable enough to infer; this is preferable to eight near-consecutive repetitions of one passage, but long-term benefit remains unproven by the contextual-diversity experiment.
- **[Product inference]** Treat encounter count as one input, not a mastery threshold. Ten contextual encounters did not yield complete knowledge in Webb’s task, and different knowledge dimensions changed at different rates.
- **[Product inference]** Record review evidence by item, direction, context, response correctness, latency or effort proxy, and elapsed time. Those fields preserve the distinctions manipulated in the experiments without pretending the studies supply a production-ready scheduling equation.
